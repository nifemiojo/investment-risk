## Zooming back out

The derivation answers **how** to calculate component contribution. The artifact design needs to answer a more important question:

> **What decision becomes easier for the PM because this decomposition exists?**

I think the current design is broadly on the right track, but I would sharpen the artifact around the workflow rather than around the metric.

## The attribution artifact’s place in the workflow

```text
Portfolio-level risk
        │
        ▼
Snapshot
"Does this portfolio need investigation?"
        │
        ▼
Attribution
"Where is the risk concentrated, and which positions should I examine first?"
        │
        ▼
Change / Drift analysis
"Why is that position carrying this risk, and is it inconsistent with the intended allocation?"
        │
        ▼
Rebalance recommendation
"What trade, if any, should I make?"
```

That gives Attribution a clear role:

> **It narrows the PM’s attention from the portfolio to the positions that explain its current risk profile.**

It is therefore not primarily a mathematical report. It is a **position-level investigation-routing artifact**.

## The decision it should support

I would state the decision as:

> **Given a portfolio whose risk warrants review, which positions should the PM investigate first, and what should they understand about each position’s role in the portfolio’s risk?**

This is slightly richer than simply:

> “Which positions do I look at?”

The PM needs to know not only *where to look*, but also *what kind of thing they are looking at*.

For example:

- Is SPY carrying disproportionate risk because it is large?
- Is it carrying disproportionate risk because it is unusually volatile?
- Is it amplifying the risk of the rest of the portfolio through correlation?
- Is IEF apparently low-risk because it is low-volatility, or because it is offsetting equity risk?
- Is GLD contributing little risk, or actively reducing it?

The attribution ranking routes attention. The surrounding fields explain the nature of the contribution.

## The PM’s actual review sequence

A useful attribution artifact should support this sequence:

### 1. Is risk concentrated?

> “Is most of the portfolio’s risk being generated by one or two positions?”

This is where the top-one and top-two summaries help.

But the artifact should be careful with the word *concentrated*. It can describe concentration; it should not decide whether that concentration is unacceptable.

### 2. Which positions dominate?

> “Which assets account for the largest share of current structural risk?”

This is the core component-contribution ranking.

The important comparison is not simply:

```text
SPY contributes 62% of risk
```

It is:

```text
SPY is 40% of capital but 62% of risk
```

The weight provides the reference that makes the contribution interpretable.

### 3. Which positions are disproportionate?

> “Does the risk profile look materially different from the capital allocation?”

This is the hidden-concentration question.

A position whose contribution is much greater than its weight deserves attention. But again, this is a prioritisation signal, not proof that the position is wrong. The PM may deliberately want a large equity risk allocation.

### 4. Which positions are diversifying?

> “Which positions are offsetting portfolio risk?”

Negative contribution is not an error. It is potentially the most important information in the report.

For example:

```text
IEF: 25% of capital, -3% of volatility contribution
```

That may indicate that IEF is doing exactly what it was included to do. A naive loss-day attribution could instead show that bonds fell on a particular stress day and incorrectly identify them as a risk source.

### 5. What should I investigate next?

The artifact should end by routing the PM to the next relevant question:

- **Change report** — has this contribution changed materially?
- **Drift analysis** — is the current allocation different from target?
- **Diversification analysis** — has the relationship between assets changed?
- **Rebalance analysis** — what would happen if the weight changed?

It should not jump directly to:

> “Reduce SPY.”

That requires target allocation, conviction, constraints, transaction costs, liquidity and potentially market views.

## What Attribution owns — and what it does not

| Question | Attribution? | Reason |
|---|---:|---|
| Where does current structural risk live? | Yes | Current weights and covariance are sufficient |
| Which positions should I investigate first? | Yes | The ranking is the artifact’s primary output |
| Is the portfolio diversified in risk terms? | Partly | It can show concentration and offsetting contributions |
| Is the current concentration acceptable? | No | Requires mandate, targets and PM judgement |
| Has the risk contribution changed? | No | Requires multiple snapshots |
| Is the allocation off target? | No | Belongs to drift analysis |
| What happens if I reduce a position? | Not as the main output | Requires marginal contribution and rebalance analysis |
| What trade should I execute? | No | Requires constraints, costs and implementation logic |
| What caused a particular historical loss? | No, not by itself | Requires a separate realised-event or forensic decomposition |

This distinction is important because the artifact should not make a stronger claim than its information set allows.

## The biggest design boundary: structural risk versus realised loss

The completed derivation supports a **structural volatility attribution**:

> “Over the estimation window, how does each asset contribute to the portfolio’s overall volatility, taking its relationship with the other assets into account?”

That is different from:

> “What caused the portfolio’s loss on the worst day?”

Those are both useful questions, but they are different artifacts or different views.

A single-day breakdown might say:

```text
IEF contributed positively to the loss because it fell that day.
```

The covariance-based attribution might say:

```text
IEF has a negative structural contribution because it generally offsets equity risk.
```

Both statements can be true. They answer different questions.

So I would change the artifact’s language to make the distinction unmistakable:

> **Structural risk attribution: where current portfolio volatility comes from.**

Rather than allowing the PM to interpret it as:

> **Historical VaR attribution: why the headline VaR number is high.**

This also makes the non-reconciliation to historical VaR less surprising. The artifact is not failing to explain the VaR number; it is answering a related but distinct decision question.

## What should be core versus supporting information?

### Core

These directly support the prioritisation decision:

- Portfolio volatility
- Asset weight
- Component contribution percentage
- Component contribution in risk units
- Contribution-minus-weight contrast
- Sorted ranking
- Positive and negative contributors

### Supporting diagnosis

These help the PM understand *why* the ranking looks the way it does:

- Standalone volatility
- Covariance with the portfolio
- Diversification contribution
- Correlation context, if presented carefully

### Downstream information

These may be computed by the engine but should probably not define the first attribution display:

- Marginal contribution to volatility
- Marginal VaR
- Hypothetical weight changes
- Trade impact
- Target comparisons

The question is not whether MCTR is useful. It clearly is. The question is whether including it in Attribution would blur the boundary between:

```text
Where is risk?
```

and:

```text
What happens if I change the portfolio?
```

My current preference would be:

- Keep CTR as the primary attribution output.
- Make MCTR available as the explicit next-step detail when the PM moves into rebalance analysis.
- Avoid making the first artifact a combined “attribution plus rebalance calculator.”

## One metric I would revisit before implementation

I would pause on:

```text
Σ standalone volatility − portfolio volatility
```

as the headline “diversification benefit.”

It is intuitive, but the unweighted sum of standalone volatilities is not directly comparable to portfolio volatility in a clean portfolio-allocation sense. A small position and a large position contribute equally to that sum if their standalone volatilities are equal, even though their portfolio relevance is very different.

That does not mean the diagnostic is useless. It means we should decide whether it is:

1. a deliberately simple illustrative diagnostic,
2. a weighted diversification measure, or
3. something that should be left out of the first version.

The core attribution does not depend on this metric. The artifact can still be strong if we defer the diversification summary until its interpretation is settled.

## A cleaner design statement

I would currently frame the artifact as follows:

> **Attribution is a point-in-time, asset-level decomposition of structural portfolio volatility. It ranks the positions that generate or offset portfolio risk and gives the PM enough context to prioritise further investigation. It describes the current risk profile; it does not judge whether that profile is acceptable or prescribe trades.**

And the PM-facing question becomes:

> **Where is the portfolio’s structural risk concentrated, which assets are diversifying it, and which positions should be examined first?**

That gives the artifact a meaningful business role without asking it to do the work of Drift Analysis or Rebalance Recommendation.

## The design test before implementation

Before writing the engine, I would want the artifact to pass this scenario:

> The Snapshot says the portfolio needs investigation. The PM opens Attribution and can, within seconds:
>
> 1. identify the dominant risk contributors;
> 2. see whether their risk share is disproportionate to their capital weight;
> 3. identify the assets acting as diversifiers;
> 4. understand whether the portfolio is effectively an equity concentration despite appearing multi-asset;
> 5. know which position or question to investigate next;
> 6. avoid mistaking a one-day stress outcome for a persistent portfolio relationship.

If it does that, the derivation is serving a decision rather than merely being displayed.

My recommendation is therefore to finalise the design around **“structural risk concentration and investigation prioritisation”**, and to treat the following as separate design decisions rather than implementation details:

- whether the historical-VaR trigger and structural-volatility attribution are clear enough together;
- whether the diversification-benefit summary is mathematically and decisionally useful;
- whether MCTR is a linked downstream view or part of the same display;
- how much descriptive interpretation the artifact should generate versus leaving the PM with raw ranked evidence.
